# Finiteness on planes and on surfaces avoiding the cycle lines

In each residual square cycle, every fixed plane contains only finitely many
primitive high cores, and its candidates are effectively enumerable.
Finiteness also holds for any fixed irreducible surface over
\(\mathbb Q\) that contains none of the eight cycle lines defined below.
In particular, fixing any one signed reduced cofactor ratio in any of the
three systems leaves finitely many primitive cores. For a fixed cofactor
denominator there are finitely many possible signed numerators by highness,
so the assertion also holds with one cofactor denominator fixed.

Write the core as \(x=(A,Q,C,D)\). The index \(i\) is cyclic modulo four.
Use the integers \(a_i,b_i\) in the
[curve theorem](SQUARE_CYCLE_CURVE_FINITE.md), and put
\(N_i=a_i^2x_{i+1}^2-b_i^2x_{i-1}^2\). The eight cycle lines are
\[
  x_i=0,\qquad a_i x_{i+1}\pm b_i x_{i-1}=0.
\]
They are the reduced support of the four complete intersections
\((x_i,N_i)\). The core coordinates satisfy \(x_i\mid N_i(x)\) and
\[
 \frac{\prod_i x_i}{\operatorname{lcm}_i(x_i)}=\epsilon,\qquad
 \epsilon\mid 2.
\]
Indeed the adjacent pairs are coprime, opposite gcds divide \(r-1\) for
119772 and divide \(2\) for 606897, and both opposite gcds cannot be even.
All coordinates are positive, with \(\max x_i<4\min x_i\).

## Bounded discrete values of a polynomial quotient

Let \(F\) define a surface of degree \(d\), and put
\(S=\mathbb Q[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]\), \(I_i=(x_i,N_i)\).
Suppose \(P\) is a homogeneous polynomial of degree \(4t\) satisfying
\[
  P\in I_i^t+(F)\quad\text{for each }i,\qquad
  P\notin \mathbb Q (x_0x_1x_2x_3)^t+(F).
\]
Choose an integer \(K>0\) clearing the coefficients of \(P\) and of
its four ideal expressions, with each generator \(x_i,N_i\) integral.
At a core on \(F=0\), each term in the expression for \(KP(x)\)
then contains \(t\) factors divisible by \(x_i\). Hence
\[
 \frac{P(x)}{(x_0x_1x_2x_3)^t}\in\frac{1}{K2^t}\mathbb Z .
\]
The absolute value of this quotient is bounded by
\(4^{4t}\|P\|_1\), where \(\|P\|_1\) is the sum of the absolute
coefficients. There are finitely many levels. At each level \(c\)
the equation \(P-c(x_0x_1x_2x_3)^t=0\) cuts a proper closed subset
of the irreducible surface. Each such subset has dimension at most
one. The curve theorem now gives finiteness.

## Existence of the quotient when the surface contains no cycle line

Assume that \(F\) does not vanish identically on any of the eight lines.
Multiplication by \(F\) on \(S/I_i\) is injective. Indeed
\(S/I_i\) embeds in the product of the two line rings, since the
two different linear factors of \(N_i\) in \(S/(x_i)\) are coprime
as factors. Multiplication by \(F\) is also injective on \(S/I_i^t\).
For this, filter it by the powers of \(I_i\). The successive
quotients \(I_i^s/I_i^{s+1}\), for \(0\le s<t\), are free over
\(S/I_i\) on the classes \(x_i^{s-j}N_i^j\), \(0\le j\le s\).
One direct verification expands in \(x_i\), since \(N_i\) does
not involve \(x_i\): at \(x_i\)-exponent \(l\le s\) the
coefficient ideal for \(I_i^s\) is generated by \(N_i^{s-l}\).
Passing to \(I_i^{s+1}\) changes it to \(N_i^{s+1-l}\).
This gives the claimed homogeneous basis and the injectivity.

In degree \(n\ge0\), \(S/I_i\) has dimension \(2n+1\), from
the polynomial ring in the three variables other than \(x_i\)
modulo one quadratic. The graded pieces for the basis above
have shifts \(s+j\). Take \(t=d\), \(n=4d\); both \(n\) and
\(n-d\) exceed every shift. Multiplication by \(F\) therefore gives
\[
 \dim\big(S/(I_i^t+(F))\big)_n
 =\sum_{0\le j\le s<t}2d=d\,t(t+1).
\]
The dimension in degree \(4d\) of \(S/(F)\) is
\(\binom{4d+3}{3}-\binom{3d+3}{3}\). Imposing the four ideal
conditions removes at most \(4d^2(d+1)\) dimensions. The
remaining dimension is at least
\[
 \frac{13d^3+18d^2+11d}{6}\ge 7.
\]
The class of \((x_0x_1x_2x_3)^d\) spans at most one dimension,
so a suitable \(P\) exists and can be obtained by rational linear
algebra. The resulting list of curves is computable. The theorem
on them proves finiteness, with effective exhaustion established
when their normalizations admit a rational parametrization over
\(\mathbb Q\).

## Planes containing cycle lines

A coordinate plane has no high core. Any other plane contains at most
one cycle line in each of the residual systems, as follows. Write
its equation \(\sum f_i x_i=0\). Containing a cycle line of index
\(i\) requires \(f_{i+2}=0\) and \((f_{i+1},f_{i-1})\) either
both zero or in one of the two nonzero signed ratios
\(\pm a_i/b_i\). A noncoordinate plane has at least two nonzero
coefficients. If it has three, at most one index works. If it
has two, they must be opposite to contain a cycle line. The
two eligible indices would require the same absolute ratio for
the coefficients of the same two variables. This fails since
\(\alpha\ne\beta\) and \(\gamma\ne\delta\) in both shapes,
in the notation of the [cofactor theorem](SQUARE_CYCLE_COFACTOR_BOUND.md).

In degree four the plane has a 15-dimensional space of polynomial
classes. The condition \(P\in I_i+(F)\) on the line \(x_i=0\)
inside the plane costs at most two linear conditions when the
restricted \(N_i\) is nonzero, and at most five if it vanishes
identically. The latter occurs exactly when the plane contains
a cycle line of that index. Thus the four conditions leave
dimension at least \(15-(5+3\cdot2)=4\), which gives an independent
quotient with \(t=1\). For example
\(h(D-\alpha'Q)=k A\), with a fixed signed rational ratio \(k/h\),
defines one of these planes. The same applies at the other three
moduli.

## An effective quadratic descent in a plane

A degree-two quotient suffices on every noncoordinate plane. In
degree two, the plane has six independent classes. Where the
restricted \(N_i\) is nonzero, the condition \(R\in I_i+(F)\)
costs two dimensions. Choose a pair \(i,j\) for which neither
index has a vanishing restricted \(N_i\). The two conditions
leave dimension at least two, so there is a homogeneous
quadratic \(R\) whose class is independent of \(x_ix_j\).
The chosen plane contains at most one cycle line, so such a
pair exists. Choose \(K>0\) clearing its ideal expressions.
At an admissible core on the plane,
\[
 \frac{R(x)}{x_ix_j}\in\frac{1}{2K}\mathbb Z,\qquad
 \left|\frac{R(x)}{x_ix_j}\right|\le16\|R\|_1.
\]
Each possible level gives a nonzero homogeneous quadratic on
the plane. Its rational locus consists of lines, exceptional
points of degenerate quadratics, or a smooth conic.
For a smooth conic, decide rational solubility by the conic
algorithm, then use a point to parametrize it by binary forms.
The [Cremona–Rusin algorithm](https://johncremona.github.io/papers/conics.pdf)
provides both steps. The declared chamber and the effective
parameter bound in the curve theorem give a terminating
enumeration. The four core equations, the fifth-state shift
and the ten-edge CRT then decide each candidate exactly.
A reducible or singular quadratic is handled by factoring or
diagonalization, with rational points on conjugate nonrational
lines confined to their intersection.

At the known 119772 factor-three core, the ratio
\((D-3Q)/A=-16/7\) defines the plane \(7D=21Q-16A\),
which contains one cycle line at \(A=0\). Use the pair \(Q,C\)
and
\[
 R=32(C^2-A^2)+84AQ-49Q^2.
\]
On \(Q=0\) it is \(32N_Q\). On \(C=0\), the plane equation
gives \(8R=-49(D^2-Q^2)\), so it also satisfies the required
ideal condition at \(C\). The known core gives \(R/(CQ)=149/8\),
verified by exact substitution.

This strengthens the opposite-pair cofactor bound in one respect:
on any unbounded sequence of primitive cores the smaller of the
two sign cofactors tends to infinity at **each** of the four
moduli. This is an effective finiteness theorem per fixed cofactor
value. A uniform bound on those values remains open.
If an infinite set of cores has
Zariski closure of dimension two, every two-dimensional
irreducible component over \(\mathbb Q\) of that closure contains
at least one cycle line. The argument still allows dimension
three and does not settle existence or uniqueness in the full
integer chamber.
